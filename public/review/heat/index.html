STORY / FICTION

The Room After Work

Maya, Rafiq and Nila are fictional. Their circumstances are synthesized from direct Bangladesh evidence, comparable knowledge and ordinary human behaviour. This is not testimony.

By the time Maya reaches the stairwell, the heat of the factory has changed shape but not disappeared. Outside it was traffic, concrete and wet air. Inside the flat it has been waiting in the walls.

Nila is on the floor beside the doorway with a workbook open. School has been disrupted by the heat. In this story, that means she is home before Maya expected her.

Rafiq has moved the small fan so it points toward Nila. Maya stands in front of it anyway for ten seconds. Nila protests. Maya laughs, then gives it back.

There is rice to wash. Maya sits first. Her shirt is still damp at the collar. Rafiq asks whether she will take tomorrow off if the night stays this hot.

Maya asks whether tomorrow's pay will take itself off too.

It is not a speech. It is arithmetic.

A missed day can mean less money. Going in can mean carrying fatigue back through the same door tomorrow night.

Later, the three arrange themselves around the moving air. Nila falls asleep first. Maya and Rafiq talk quietly about whether one of them can change hours if school closes again. They disagree about what is practical, then stop because they are too tired to keep disagreeing.

Nothing catastrophic happens. That is the point. The heat does not need a dramatic ending to reorganise a day. It can take a little work, a little money, a little patience and a little sleep, then come back tomorrow.

FICTION BOUNDARY

The household, dialogue and events are invented. Heat exposure, worker strain, income risk, hot homes and school disruption are evidence-established conditions. Comparative knowledge expands what STORY may plausibly connect; it does not turn fiction into fact.

EVIDENCE + BOUNDARIES

Inspect the evidence.

KNOWN

Persistent heat exposure; worker health/productivity burden; missed work and income loss in surveyed groups; home heat exposure in sampled workers; school disruption and child heat risk; tested adaptation options.

NOT CLAIMED

That every worker experiences the same symptoms; that every household loses the same income; that heat alone caused all school disruption; that Maya's family exists; or that cooling investments guarantee returns.

SR-H01 · World Bank — Bangladesh heat and economic risk

Supports: long-run warming, Dhaka heat-index burden, health effects, estimated 250 million lost workdays and economic loss in 2024.

Limitation: national estimates do not describe every worker or household.

SR-H02 · IIED — No escaping the heat

Supports: 175 Dhaka informal-worker households; workdays and income lost; health burden; hot rented homes and limited recovery.

Supports: eight Dhaka-region apparel factories, three dozen worker homes, seven-month heat-stress season and cooling-investment estimates.

Limitation: factory findings and payback estimates are study-specific.

SR-H04 · UNICEF — Learning Interrupted / Bangladesh

Supports: 35 million Bangladeshi children experienced schooling disruption from climate-related hazards in 2024; April–May heatwaves forced nationwide school closure for up to two weeks.

Limitation: the 35 million total includes multiple hazards, not heat alone.
