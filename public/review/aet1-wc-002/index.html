When the Heat Comes Inside: How Rising Temperatures Became a Persistent Constraint in Dhaka

FACT
Heat in Bangladesh has moved beyond a string of isolated, extreme days to become a persistent physical and economic force. New evidence from 2026 illustrates that this shift now touches work, home, health, and the prospects of the next generation in Dhaka and beyond, transforming daily life and livelihoods.

The Heat

Heat as a Persistent Condition

FACT
Heat in Bangladesh is no longer limited to isolated, extraordinary days. Analysis of more than 16,000 people found a long-run trend of rising temperatures, with increasing physical and mental health impacts. This evidence indicates that what was once rare is now becoming an expected and routine condition, fundamentally altering the experience of work and daily life.

WB-HEAT-2025

The Urban Heat Burden: City Shape and Humidity

FACT
In Dhaka, the dense urban form and persistently high humidity amplify the heat burden beyond the recorded air temperature. National-scale analysis shows that humidity and urban density together increase the discomfort and health risks people feel—a phenomenon not captured by temperature readings alone. These conditions are present citywide but vary by location and housing within Dhaka.

The Shift

Heat Enters the Working Day

FACT
Worker interviews and household surveys reveal that rising temperatures bring significant physical strain to the working day. Documented experiences include headaches, dizziness, fatigue, and nausea on the job, as well as absenteeism directly tied to heat exposure. Hydration breaks and time spent cooling down are necessary but reduce productive work hours, forcing a trade-off between health and earnings for many laborers.

RMG-WORKER-2025 IIED-HEAT-2026

Factories and the Long Heat Season

FACT
In sampled apparel factories around Dhaka, year-long evidence documents that the heat season now stretches for seven months. These factories are not insulated from the outdoor climate: indoor temperatures can mirror or even intensify outdoor heat, persisting through shifts and into the evening. The result is prolonged exposure for workers, challenging both health and productivity.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026

Outdoor Work: The Cost of Avoiding Heat

FACT
For informal outdoor workers in Dhaka, such as rickshaw pullers and construction laborers, the only immediate way to avoid heat is often to reduce hours or miss work entirely. Surveys specifically show that heat avoidance translates into lost paid time and income, with direct consequences for worker households surveyed during the study period.

The Pay Packet

Lost Workdays and Household Budgets

FACT
Surveyed informal worker households in Dhaka report that heat-induced absenteeism and reduced work capacity directly pressure household budgets. Missed shifts or shorter working days diminish income, leaving affected households to manage expenses with less. While these losses are evident within the survey group, the precise scale for the entire city is not established.

IIED-HEAT-2026

National Scale: Heat, Work, and the Economy

FACT
At the national level, modeled estimates for 2024 attribute approximately 250 million lost workdays to heat exposure. This represents both a macroeconomic challenge for Bangladesh and a reflection of the individual experiences documented in Dhaka. The figure synthesizes diverse data, but cannot be broken down to individual households or workplaces without further study.

The Home

The Home as Heat Zone

FACT
The end of a work shift does not necessarily mean relief from heat. Evidence from sampled worker homes and surveyed households in Dhaka shows that high temperatures often persist through the evening and night, leaving residents without respite. Both recovery and sleep may be compromised, extending heat’s effects beyond the workplace and into all hours of daily life.

Inequality in Heat Recovery

UNCERTAIN
Who can recover from heat, and how quickly, depends largely on the quality of housing, access to cooling, and household resources. Evidence suggests marked differences in coping capacity across the surveyed population. However, the exact strategies households use and their related expenditures are not fully documented for each local community or home, leaving open questions about the scale and specific pathways of adaptation.

The Next Generation

Heat, Health, and Schooling Disruption for Children

FACT
Nationwide, heatwaves have contributed to school closures and have disrupted the health and learning of children. UNICEF’s evidence for 2024 confirms that Bangladesh experienced significant child health and education setbacks during climate-related school suspensions, of which heatwaves were a leading but not the only factor.

UNICEF-EDU-2025

Unresolved Household Impacts of Education Disruption

UNCERTAIN
While it is clear that school interruption due to heat places an added burden on households and children, details about the precise consequences—such as extra childcare, learning loss, or family income effects—are not fully established in the current evidence bundle. The full household-level impact of closures remains unresolved.

The Fix

Physical and Operational Adaptation in Practice

FACT
Evidence from Dhaka’s apparel factories and informal worker surveys demonstrates that adaptation to heat can take the form of physical improvements—like installing shade, increasing airflow, providing cooling, and ensuring hydration—as well as operational changes such as adjusting work hours or instituting breaks. These interventions can reduce some documented burdens, with their effectiveness confirmed within the studied sites.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026 IIED-HEAT-2026

The Economics of Factory Cooling

FACT
In a subset of sampled apparel factories, investments in workplace cooling and insulation were shown to offer measurable returns. Analysis estimates payback periods for these interventions ranging from one to four years, based on reduced heat-related productivity losses and absenteeism. These figures are specific to the studied factories and do not guarantee identical outcomes elsewhere.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026

Who Gets the Fix? The Uneven Future of Adaptation

UNCERTAIN
While practical responses to heat exist, evidence highlights a distributional dilemma: not all households, workers, or workplaces have the resources to afford protection, and some may be left with greater residual risk. Current research documents this inequality and surfaces questions about how future adaptation costs and benefits—such as access to cooling or social protection—will be shared. These allocations are not yet resolved in the available evidence.

The Room After Work

Maya, Rafiq, Nila, their flat, dialogue, schedule decisions and events are invented. The surrounding heat, worker strain, income risk, household exposure and school disruption are evidence-established conditions; comparative/ordinary-human knowledge supports the fictional connections.

By the time Maya reaches the stairwell, the heat of the factory has changed shape but not disappeared. Outside it was traffic, concrete and wet air. Inside the flat it has been waiting in the walls.

Nila is on the floor beside the doorway with a workbook open. School has been disrupted by the heat. In this story, that means she is home before Maya expected her. The closure is grounded in documented heat-related school disruption; this family's afternoon is invented.

Rafiq has moved the small fan so it points toward Nila. Maya stands in front of it anyway for ten seconds. Nila protests. Maya laughs, then gives it back.

There is rice to wash. Maya sits first. Her shirt is still damp at the collar. At work she drank water whenever she could. She has the dull heaviness that comes after a day spent negotiating with heat.

Rafiq asks whether she will take tomorrow off if the night stays this hot. Maya asks whether tomorrow's pay will take itself off too.

It is not a speech. It is arithmetic. A missed day can mean less money. Going in can mean carrying fatigue back through the same door tomorrow night.

Nila asks whether school will open. Neither adult knows. Rafiq says they will work it out. Maya looks at the fan, then at the window, then at her daughter.

Later, after the cooking, the three of them arrange themselves around the moving air. Nila falls asleep first. Maya and Rafiq talk quietly about whether one of them can change hours if school closes again. They disagree about what is practical, then stop because they are too tired to keep disagreeing.

Nothing catastrophic happens. That is the point. The heat does not need a dramatic ending to reorganise a day. It can take a little work, a little money, a little patience and a little sleep, then come back tomorrow.

These people, dialogue and events are invented. This is not testimony.

The Consequences: What Is Known, What Remains Uncertain

FACT
Direct evidence shows that rising heat in Dhaka results in missed work, reduced income, health symptoms, disrupted recovery at home, and interruptions to schooling. Surveys and interviews document physical exhaustion, headaches, dizziness, and financial strain linked to heat exposure both at work and at home. National estimates model a loss of about 250 million workdays in 2024 alone.

UNCERTAIN
However, the precise ways in which these effects add up across different households, workplaces, and schools are not fully resolved. Not all suspensions of schooling, health complaints, or income drops can be attributed to heat alone; other climate hazards and socio-economic factors play a role. The relative ability of each household to recover or adapt to repeated heat exposure remains unevenly documented.

UNCERTAIN
Some adaptive solutions have been trialed and measured—including workplace cooling, hydration practices, and social-protection efforts—but the full cost, accessibility, and distribution of these fixes are unresolved. It is not yet clear who will be able to consistently benefit from improved cooling or policy support as heat continues to intensify.

PLACE · GEOGRAPHIC CONTEXT

Dhaka and Greater Dhaka: The Urban Epicenter of Heat

FACT
Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh, serves as the geographic core for these findings. The research focuses on the experience of residents and workers in Dhaka and its surrounding areas, including its dense neighborhoods, sprawling informal settlements, and the industrial corridors where apparel factories cluster. While some sources provide national figures, the detailed evidence for rising heat's everyday effects comes from within Dhaka itself.

IIED-HEAT-2026 CORNELL-HEAT-2026 RMG-WORKER-2025 WB-HEAT-2025

FACT
Not every workplace, home, or school in Bangladesh shares identical conditions. The studies referenced here specifically sample Dhaka-region factories, worker households, informal laborers, and local schools. This means the lived heat burden described is grounded in Dhaka’s urban climate—marked by concrete, high humidity, and limited space for relief. The urban environment amplifies risks and shapes the possibilities for cooling, adaptation, and recovery.
