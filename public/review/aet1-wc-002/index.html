When the Heat Comes Inside: How Persistent Heat Has Become a Labour, Health, and Household Constraint in Dhaka, Bangladesh

FACT
In recent years, Dhaka and its surrounding regions have experienced a transformation in the nature of heat. What was once regarded as an episodic weather hazard has shifted, backed by national-scale and city-case evidence, into a persistent feature that shapes daily life, work, and health for millions. Rising temperatures are no longer only a concern on extreme days; instead, prolonged periods of high heat challenge both earning a living and recovering at home.

WB-HEAT-2025 IIED-HEAT-2026 CORNELL-HEAT-2026

FACT
This change is acutely felt in Dhaka, Bangladesh’s capital and largest city, where dense urban form and humidity amplify the burden well beyond what thermometers record. Informal workers, factory laborers, and households navigating limited resources now encounter heat as a constraint on productivity, income, health, and even children’s schooling. Surveys and factory studies in Dhaka show that both workplace and home are interconnected sites of exposure, and escaping the heat at work often means bringing the same risks and exhaustion back home.

INTERPRETATION
Not all is inevitable. New evidence from 2026 points to the capacity for practical adaptation, such as cooling interventions and targeted social protection, to reduce part of the heat’s burden in selected factories and communities. However, neither the risks nor the benefits are evenly distributed, and substantial uncertainties remain about who will access protection and who will continue to bear the greatest exposure.

FACT
It is crucial to recognize the limits of what this evidence can say. While the measurement of health impacts and workday losses is based on large samples and localized studies, not every effect can be mapped directly to climate trends alone, and not all populations or factories experience risks or benefits in the same way. Personal and household experiences vary, and the wider allocation of adaptation costs is still unresolved.

Heat Becomes a Persistent Condition

FACT
Recent measurements from Bangladesh show that rising temperatures have shifted from an exceptional danger into an ongoing part of life. National-level analysis links this persistent heat to widespread impacts on both physical and mental health, with the number of affected days increasing beyond rare peaks to include long periods of cumulative exposure.

The Shift

Heat Within the Workday

FACT
Workers in sampled Dhaka garment factories and surveyed informal sectors report physical strain, fatigue, and health symptoms as heat intensifies. Accounts include headaches, dizziness, and nausea, compounded by barriers to drinking water, hydration, and ventilation. These cumulative burdens add up over a working week and season, not just on extreme days.

Factory Walls Provide Limited Shelter

FACT
Year-round study in Dhaka-region factories reveals that indoor workspaces often track outdoor climate trends, sustaining high temperatures across a heat-stress season lasting up to seven months. Cooling within factories is often limited, so the climate outside readily becomes the baseline condition inside.

Trade-offs for Informal Outdoor Workers

FACT
Surveyed informal workers in Dhaka face a harsh dilemma: to avoid debilitating heat, they must sometimes forgo paid hours, which translates directly into reduced income. These trade-offs were captured within the sample population and period, illustrating how heat exposes vulnerabilities in the city’s most precarious livelihoods.

The Pay Packet

Household Budgets Under Pressure

FACT
For many households surveyed in Dhaka, missed work due to heat leads to an immediate and tangible loss of income. Aggregated survey results show that lost days and reduced hours impose pressure on the most basic household budgets, often forcing difficult decisions among food, rent, and other needs.

Heat as a Macroeconomic Constraint

FACT
Modeling at the national level suggests that about 250 million workdays were lost due to heat in Bangladesh in 2024 alone. This modeled estimate signals a macroeconomic burden that extends well beyond single households or neighborhoods, with cumulative implications for productivity and growth.

The Home

Heat Persists Beyond the Factory Gate

FACT
Evidence from worker homes in the Dhaka region and household surveys demonstrates that exposure does not end with the workday. Housing conditions often mean that heat follows workers into spaces meant for rest and recovery, making the boundary between workplace and home less protective than hoped.

The Next Generation

Heat Disrupts Schooling and Child Health

FACT
During Bangladesh’s extended heatwaves, school closures became widespread, with millions of children experiencing disruption to learning and an increased risk of heat-related illness. The burden on children is felt not just in academics but also in their health, as documented in 2024 statistics.

UNICEF-EDU-2025

School Interruption Shifts Burden to Homes

UNCERTAIN
While evidence links heatwave-related school closures to disruption, the consequences for household childcare and children’s longer-term learning outcomes are not fully measured in current sources. What is clear is that when schools close, the effects necessarily shift part of the heat burden onto families’ time and resources.

The Fix

Adapting: Practical Changes, Partial Relief

FACT
Tested interventions, both physical and operational, show evidence of reducing heat-related burdens in Dhaka factories and surveyed households. These include changes to shade, ventilation, access to drinking water, and modified work practices, with results varying by site and available resources.

Factory Cooling: The Promise and the Limits

FACT
In sampled apparel factories, investment in cooling and ventilation showed that it is possible to recoup costs over 1–4 years through reduced workday losses and improved productivity. However, these are site-specific estimates and do not guarantee the same payoff everywhere or under every financing arrangement.

The Distributional Challenge of Adaptation

UNCERTAIN
Adaptation to heat raises pressing questions of distribution: evidence shows that not all Dhaka households or workers have equal access to protection. Who gets cooling, who pays, and who continues to bear the residual burden remain unresolved, as does how emerging interventions might be scaled or allocated in the future.

The Room After Work

Maya, Rafiq, Nila, their flat, dialogue, schedule decisions and events are invented. The surrounding heat, worker strain, income risk, household exposure and school disruption are evidence-established conditions; comparative/ordinary-human knowledge supports the fictional connections.

By the time Maya reaches the stairwell, the heat of the factory has changed shape but not disappeared. Outside it was traffic, concrete and wet air. Inside the flat it has been waiting in the walls.

Nila is on the floor beside the doorway with a workbook open. School has been disrupted by the heat. In this story, that means she is home before Maya expected her. The closure is grounded in documented heat-related school disruption; this family's afternoon is invented.

Rafiq has moved the small fan so it points toward Nila. Maya stands in front of it anyway for ten seconds. Nila protests. Maya laughs, then gives it back.

There is rice to wash. Maya sits first. Her shirt is still damp at the collar. At work she drank water whenever she could. She has the dull heaviness that comes after a day spent negotiating with heat.

Rafiq asks whether she will take tomorrow off if the night stays this hot. Maya asks whether tomorrow's pay will take itself off too.

It is not a speech. It is arithmetic. A missed day can mean less money. Going in can mean carrying fatigue back through the same door tomorrow night.

Nila asks whether school will open. Neither adult knows. Rafiq says they will work it out. Maya looks at the fan, then at the window, then at her daughter.

Later, after the cooking, the three of them arrange themselves around the moving air. Nila falls asleep first. Maya and Rafiq talk quietly about whether one of them can change hours if school closes again. They disagree about what is practical, then stop because they are too tired to keep disagreeing.

Nothing catastrophic happens. That is the point. The heat does not need a dramatic ending to reorganise a day. It can take a little work, a little money, a little patience and a little sleep, then come back tomorrow.

These people, dialogue and events are invented. This is not testimony.

Consequences: What Changes, Who Loses, and Where Uncertainty Remains

FACT
Observed effects include measurable reductions in work capacity, health symptoms among workers, lost income in affected households, home environments that remain hot following work shifts, and educational disruption for children during national heatwaves. Reports document headaches, dizziness, and exhaustion in factories, alongside missed work and lost pay among surveyed informal workers.

FACT
At the national level, the modeled economic impact is substantial, with an estimated 250 million workdays lost to heat in 2024. Yet, the distribution of these effects is uneven. The specific coping capacity of households—for example, to afford cooling or reduce exposure—remains variable and incompletely tracked by survey or case evidence.

INTERPRETATION
Key uncertainties remain about the precise causal pathways between observed heat, economic loss, school closures, and longer-term wellbeing. National statistics aggregate many experiences and hazards, and the future allocation of resources for adaptation has yet to be determined, leaving some consequences and inequalities unresolved.

PLACE · GEOGRAPHIC CONTEXT

Dhaka, Bangladesh: The Urban Core of a Warming National Crisis

FACT
Dhaka, the bustling capital of Bangladesh, stands as the geographic focal point of current heat evidence. Surveys have targeted informal-worker households within the city, factory studies have measured conditions on its industrial floors, and schooling disruptions have radiated from urban centers into the nation’s educational landscape. While the scope of heat’s burden extends beyond the city, Dhaka’s dense architecture, urban heat island effects, and high humidity together represent a concentrated microcosm of national risks.

IIED-HEAT-2026 CORNELL-HEAT-2026 UNICEF-EDU-2025

FACT
Evidence from garment factories and informal settlements around Dhaka typifies the city experience, though it does not establish universal conditions across all workplaces or homes. The city serves here as a grounded case, not a stand-in for every town, village, or rural worker in Bangladesh.
