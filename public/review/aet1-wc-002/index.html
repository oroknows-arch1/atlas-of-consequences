When the Heat Comes Inside: An Epochal Change in Bangladesh

FACT
In Bangladesh, rising heat has shifted from being an occasional weather hazard to a persistent feature that shapes daily life, labor, and wellbeing. Evidence gathered from 2024-2026 reveals that this transformation is not simply about more hot days, but about a new normal in which heat acts as a multi-dimensional constraint—impacting work, health, household dynamics, and the broader economy. In the heart of these changes lie Dhaka and its surrounding region, a dense urban setting where heat’s effects are magnified by built environments and humidity.

FACT
Those most affected include urban and informal workers, garment-factory employees, children, and families living in rented, uninsulated homes. Heat does not stop at the factory gates; it follows people into recovery spaces, households, and schools, exposing both economic life and family routines to strain. Recent studies support that practical cooling and social protection measures can reduce at least part of this burden, though full relief depends on how adaptation is distributed—and new uncertainties arise in who benefits and who is left vulnerable.

Not all disruptions, such as school closures, can be attributed to heat alone, and the detail of household coping or the future allocation of adaptation resources is not resolved by the available studies.

The Heat

Heat as a Persistent Condition

FACT
Long-term national analysis points to heat in Bangladesh moving beyond exceptional spikes; it has become a persistent part of the environment. The health and productivity costs, measured among over 16,000 people, include substantial physical and mental health impacts and contribute to enormous annual losses in work time. This transformation signals that populations are now navigating a prolonged heat season rather than isolated episodes.

WB-HEAT-2025

Urban Heat: Dense Cities and Humidity Intensify the Challenge

FACT
In Dhaka, the human impact of heat is shaped not just by temperature, but by humidity and concentrated urban forms. Humidity increases the heat index, amplifying how the warmth feels and raising health risks even when air temperature alone may not seem extreme. Such compounded effects are now recognized as a major factor in the burden reported by city residents.

The Shift

Heat on the Factory Floor and in the Street

FACT
Surveyed workers in Dhaka’s garment factories and informal sectors report significant physical strain, headaches, dizziness, and dehydration during periods of high heat. Evidence links these symptoms to absenteeism and productivity declines, as workers must choose between enduring exposure or taking breaks—which can mean lost income. Hydration and cooling remain challenges, with barriers documented in both factory and street work environments.

RMG-WORKER-2025 IIED-HEAT-2026

Factories Do Not Block Out the Heat

FACT
Even indoors, in sampled Dhaka-region apparel factories, workers experience a seven-month heat-stress season. Year-long monitoring shows that environmental controls and building structures are often insufficient to keep indoor conditions safe and comfortable. While the evidence is specific to the factories studied and not all such workplaces nationwide, it underscores the porous barrier between the outdoor climate and people’s working environments.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026

Informal Workers Face a Trade-Off: Heat or Lost Income

FACT
For informal outdoor workers in Dhaka, the burden of heat goes beyond discomfort—it often translates directly to lost paid hours. Survey data from 175 households reveal that avoidance of extreme conditions comes at an economic cost borne most heavily by those with the least resilience. The reported effects are specific to these surveyed workers and the period of study.

The Pay Packet

Heat Translates into Household Income Pressure

FACT
Missed work and reduced productivity are not only workplace problems; they affect entire households. Surveyed families reliant on daily wages must manage budget shortfalls when one or more working members lose income due to heat-related illness or absence. While quantitative detail applies to the studied populations, the pressure on income security is a documented pattern.

IIED-HEAT-2026

National Economic Impact: Hundreds of Millions of Days Lost

FACT
At a national scale, estimated models for 2024 indicate that Bangladesh lost about 250 million workdays to heat exposure. While these figures are modeled, rather than tallied from every workplace, they represent a major macroeconomic constraint—reflecting how persistent heat now shapes the country’s economic outcomes.

The Home

After Work, the Heat Follows Home

FACT
The end of a work shift does not mark the end of heat exposure for many in Dhaka. Sampled evidence from worker homes reflects that, for many families, rented and informal dwellings retain heat, and recovery is slow or incomplete. This continuous cycle of exposure affects household vitality and the ability to rest before the next workday.

Unequal Capacity for Recovery at Home

UNCERTAIN
Not all households can recover from heat in the same way. Evidence shows differences in access to cooling, quality of housing, and available resources. However, the details of how households seek relief or allocate spending remains unresolved in this evidence set, and further locality or case-specific data would be needed for concrete narration.

The Next Generation

Children’s Health and Learning Disrupted by Heat

FACT
Heatwaves in 2024 led to school closures across Bangladesh, contributing to interruptions of both learning and child health. While school closures result from multiple hazards, heat plays a notable and documented role, making children and families part of this widening web of heat impact.

The Fix

Adapting to Heat: Measured and Practical Responses

FACT
Interventions such as increased airflow, shade, cooling technologies, hydration supports, and adjustments in work schedules have been tested in both home and factory environments. Evidence from surveyed workplaces and households indicates that these measures can reduce part of the physical and economic burden, although their extent and uptake are not universal.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026 IIED-HEAT-2026

Cooling Investments: Economic Return, With Caveats

FACT
In the sampled apparel factories, certain investments in cooling and ventilation demonstrated the potential for financial payback within one to four years through reduced heat-related losses. These findings are specific to the studied settings and do not guarantee similar returns elsewhere.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026

Who Gets Protected? Adaptation and Unequal Exposure

UNCERTAIN
The ability to afford or access effective adaptation varies greatly. While improved cooling and social protection can mitigate some heat effects, documented evidence points to persistent inequality in who is shielded and who continues to bear the brunt of rising temperatures. The future of how adaptation resources and costs will be allocated across urban populations remains unresolved, forming an ongoing policy and social challenge.

The Room After Work

Maya, Rafiq, Nila, their flat, dialogue, schedule decisions and events are invented. The surrounding heat, worker strain, income risk, household exposure and school disruption are evidence-established conditions; comparative/ordinary-human knowledge supports the fictional connections.

By the time Maya reaches the stairwell, the heat of the factory has changed shape but not disappeared. Outside it was traffic, concrete and wet air. Inside the flat it has been waiting in the walls.

Nila is on the floor beside the doorway with a workbook open. School has been disrupted by the heat. In this story, that means she is home before Maya expected her. The closure is grounded in documented heat-related school disruption; this family's afternoon is invented.

Rafiq has moved the small fan so it points toward Nila. Maya stands in front of it anyway for ten seconds. Nila protests. Maya laughs, then gives it back.

There is rice to wash. Maya sits first. Her shirt is still damp at the collar. At work she drank water whenever she could. She has the dull heaviness that comes after a day spent negotiating with heat.

Rafiq asks whether she will take tomorrow off if the night stays this hot. Maya asks whether tomorrow's pay will take itself off too.

It is not a speech. It is arithmetic. A missed day can mean less money. Going in can mean carrying fatigue back through the same door tomorrow night.

Nila asks whether school will open. Neither adult knows. Rafiq says they will work it out. Maya looks at the fan, then at the window, then at her daughter.

Later, after the cooking, the three of them arrange themselves around the moving air. Nila falls asleep first. Maya and Rafiq talk quietly about whether one of them can change hours if school closes again. They disagree about what is practical, then stop because they are too tired to keep disagreeing.

Nothing catastrophic happens. That is the point. The heat does not need a dramatic ending to reorganise a day. It can take a little work, a little money, a little patience and a little sleep, then come back tomorrow.

FICTION BOUNDARY

These people, dialogue and events are invented. This is not testimony.

CONSEQUENCES · FACT AND UNCERTAINTY

Consequences: What Is Known and What Remains Uncertain

FACT
Observed effects include widespread health symptoms among workers, large-scale lost workdays, income pressure on affected households, and learning and wellbeing impacts for children during heatwaves and school closures. Studies also find that both workplace and home environments contribute to the intensity of heat exposure, and that tested cooling measures can mitigate some of these effects in sampled environments.

WB-HEAT-2025 RMG-WORKER-2025 IIED-HEAT-2026 CORNELL-HEAT-2026 UNICEF-EDU-2025

UNCERTAIN
Uncertain or unresolved issues include the exact scale of household coping gaps, differences in adaptation capacity across neighborhoods, the lasting impact of disrupted schooling on family income or learning, and how future adaptation costs and benefits will be distributed. Evidence supports inequality in exposure and recovery, but does not specify household-level future outcomes or policy responses.

Dhaka, Bangladesh: Urban Heat at the Heart of the Evidence

FACT
The core of this documented change is Dhaka, Bangladesh’s largest city, along with its surrounding region. Study sources encompass sampled apparel factories, informal-worker households, and schools in Dhaka, reflecting both the city’s dense urban landscape and the daily movements of its residents. While the evidence builds a detailed picture of urban and peri-urban experience, it does not claim to represent all rural or distant communities across the country.
