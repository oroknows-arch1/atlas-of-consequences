When the Heat Comes Inside

When the Heat Comes Inside

FACT
Dhaka, Bangladesh faces a heat transformation: what was once seen as an episodic weather hazard now persists as a labor, health, household, and economic constraint. Recent 2026 studies reveal that heat is no longer limited to rare, extreme hot days but has become a persistent burden, directly affecting daily life and the functioning of key sectors. Across the city’s workers, families, and children, the impacts reach from the factory floor and rickshaw routes, into crowded homes, and all the way to classrooms.

FACT
This change is anchored in extensive local and national analysis covering health, work, and household resilience. Surveys of Dhaka households and workplaces, interviews with garment workers, national workday modeling, and records of educational disruption draw a picture covering both formal and informal sectors. The research makes clear that heat’s burden is uneven, and while new interventions in cooling and social protection show promise, not every solution works everywhere, nor is every consequence fully understood.

The Heat

A Lasting Heat Season

FACT
Over recent years, heat in Bangladesh has shifted from a sporadic challenge to a persistent presence. Rather than disrupting life only during brief heatwaves, rising temperatures now extend across a prolonged season, pressing into daily work and home routines for months on end. National studies involving over sixteen thousand people show that this is no longer a matter of an occasional hazard but a lasting change that alters how work, rest, and health play out in Dhaka and across the country.

WB-HEAT-2025

City Heat: More Than Just Temperature

FACT
Dhaka’s dense urban form amplifies heat risk through accumulated warmth in concrete, limited airflow, and high humidity, which increases the physical heat felt by residents. Even when air temperatures remain similar, humidity intensifies the burden on the human body, extending discomfort and health strain. The scale of this effect is shaped by the city's structure, making the urban heat index a significant aspect of daily life that goes beyond the thermometer’s reading.

The Shift

Heat at Work

FACT
For Dhaka’s workers, heat directly enters the working day. Garment workers and informal laborers contend with physical strain, increased need for hydration, unscheduled breaks, and, in some cases, an inability to finish the day’s work. Documented interviews and surveys point to frequent symptoms of heat stress, including headaches, dizziness, and fatigue, along with missed days and lost income. These effects are not attributed to a single day or individual but have become a measurable pattern affecting many in the labor force.

RMG-WORKER-2025 IIED-HEAT-2026

Indoor Heat Persists

FACT
Evidence from a year-long study of Dhaka-region apparel factories found that heat is not left at the factory gate. Even indoors, high temperatures can persist across a seven-month heat-stress season. The sampled factories showed that external climate translates to indoor conditions, with workers facing sustained exposure throughout much of the year. However, these findings are specific to the sampled sites and do not establish that all factories experience these conditions.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026

For Informal Workers, Heat Means Hard Trade-Offs

FACT
For informal outdoor workers, avoiding heat often comes at the cost of lost paid hours. Surveyed households reported a direct link between extreme heat and missed work, as some workers choose or are forced to reduce hours or skip shifts entirely. The result is an immediate pressure on household finances, recorded for the population covered by the survey and not extrapolated to all workers in the city.

The Pay Packet

Income Pressures from Lost Work

FACT
When heat forces workers to miss days or reduce their capacity, the impact is felt directly in the pay packet. Surveyed informal-worker households in Dhaka experienced missed work translating into lost income, with the heat burden carrying through to budgets, nutrition, and essentials. These losses are measured within the surveyed group and cannot be assumed for all households in the region.

IIED-HEAT-2026

Heat and the Economy: National Scale Losses

FACT
The economic impact of lost work mounts quickly at a national scale. Modeling based on survey and health analysis estimated that Bangladesh lost roughly 250 million workdays to heat in 2024. This modeled figure is not a direct measurement of every workplace, but it is a clear indicator of how persistent heat now acts as a macroeconomic constraint.

The Home

Homes as Part of the Heat System

FACT
The end of a shift does not guarantee relief from heat. Evidence from sampled worker homes and household surveys confirms that for many in Dhaka, indoor spaces remain hot well into the evening and night. The challenge of recovering from daytime exposure is compounded by crowded living arrangements and limited cooling, linking the workplace and the home in a single, ongoing heat system.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026 IIED-HEAT-2026

Unequal Recovery and Cooling Access

UNCERTAIN
Not all Dhaka households cope with heat in the same way or have equal access to resources that could lessen its toll. Studies show that recovery from heat exposure depends on factors like quality of housing, ability to afford cooling or ventilation, household size, and schedules. Some surveyed households report significant difficulty in recuperating before the next day’s heat arrives. However, evidence does not cover every strategy or expenditure for all localities, leaving unresolved how coping varies across the city.

The Next Generation

Heat and the Next Generation

FACT
Children in Bangladesh encounter the consequences of rising heat through increased health risks and disruption to their education. In 2024, heatwaves contributed to school closures across the country, and affected children faced interrupted learning and heightened vulnerability during extreme heat periods. While school disruption is caused by several climate-related hazards, heat was a documented factor in these closures.

UNICEF-EDU-2025

Burden Shifts to Households and Children

UNCERTAIN
When schools close due to heat, some of the resulting burden shifts back to households, affecting childcare and the use of children's time. However, there is not enough direct evidence to quantify impacts on household income, learning loss, or childcare arrangements for individual families. The ripple effects of interruptions remain an area for further research.

The Fix

How Adaptation Begins

FACT
Efforts to adapt to heat are already measurable in Dhaka's workplaces and homes. Tested interventions include increased shading, improved airflow, provision of water and hydration breaks, and adjustments to work practices. Some factories and surveyed households have adopted these physical and operational changes, with recently collected evidence highlighting the benefits in worker comfort and reduced strain within the measured samples.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026 IIED-HEAT-2026

Investing in Cooling: Measured Returns

FACT
Studies in selected Dhaka-region apparel factories demonstrate that certain cooling investments, such as modern ventilation or cooling installations, can pay for themselves through reductions in heat-related losses within one to four years. These estimates are calculated for the studied sites and reflect specific economic and physical conditions—they are not guaranteed returns for all factories or interventions.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026

Who Gets Protection, Who Bears the Heat?

UNCERTAIN
Adaptation to heat in Dhaka is also a question of distribution. Evidence confirms marked inequality in access to cooling and protective measures: some workers, factories, and households are better able to protect themselves, while others remain highly exposed. This unequal capacity now shapes vulnerability, and future allocation of adaptation costs and benefits remains an open question that current evidence cannot resolve.

STORY / FICTION

The Room After Work

Maya, Rafiq, Nila, their flat, dialogue, schedule decisions and events are invented. The surrounding heat, worker strain, income risk, household exposure and school disruption are evidence-established conditions; comparative/ordinary-human knowledge supports the fictional connections.

By the time Maya reaches the stairwell, the heat of the factory has changed shape but not disappeared. Outside it was traffic, concrete and wet air. Inside the flat it has been waiting in the walls.

Nila is on the floor beside the doorway with a workbook open. School has been disrupted by the heat. In this story, that means she is home before Maya expected her. The closure is grounded in documented heat-related school disruption; this family's afternoon is invented.

Rafiq has moved the small fan so it points toward Nila. Maya stands in front of it anyway for ten seconds. Nila protests. Maya laughs, then gives it back.

There is rice to wash. Maya sits first. Her shirt is still damp at the collar. At work she drank water whenever she could. She has the dull heaviness that comes after a day spent negotiating with heat.

Rafiq asks whether she will take tomorrow off if the night stays this hot. Maya asks whether tomorrow's pay will take itself off too.

It is not a speech. It is arithmetic. A missed day can mean less money. Going in can mean carrying fatigue back through the same door tomorrow night.

Nila asks whether school will open. Neither adult knows. Rafiq says they will work it out. Maya looks at the fan, then at the window, then at her daughter.

Later, after the cooking, the three of them arrange themselves around the moving air. Nila falls asleep first. Maya and Rafiq talk quietly about whether one of them can change hours if school closes again. They disagree about what is practical, then stop because they are too tired to keep disagreeing.

Nothing catastrophic happens. That is the point. The heat does not need a dramatic ending to reorganise a day. It can take a little work, a little money, a little patience and a little sleep, then come back tomorrow.

FICTION BOUNDARY

These people, dialogue and events are invented. This is not testimony.

CONSEQUENCES · FACT AND UNCERTAINTY

Observed Effects and Uncertainties

FACT
Observed effects include significant health strain, lost workdays, missed income, and disrupted schooling due to heat exposure. For many workers, heat leads to symptoms such as headaches, dizziness, fatigue, and absenteeism, as well as lost household earnings. At the national level, 2024 saw an estimated 250 million workdays lost to heat, and recurring school closures affected child health and learning. However, this evidence is drawn from survey populations and modeled estimates, not the full national experience.

WB-HEAT-2025 IIED-HEAT-2026 RMG-WORKER-2025 UNICEF-EDU-2025

UNCERTAIN
Uncertainties remain about how every household copes with heat or how income risks are distributed. Not all Dhaka factories or families have the same ability to adapt, and while some interventions show measurable benefits, the future allocation of costs and who bears the greatest residual heat burden is unresolved. The particular outcomes of school closures and household-level effects remain areas where more precise evidence is needed.

FACT
