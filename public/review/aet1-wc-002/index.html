When the Heat Comes Inside

Heat Crosses the Threshold from Weather to Wear

FACT
In Dhaka, Bangladesh, heat has shifted from being just a weather event to an unyielding reality, with rising temperatures imposing steady new constraints on labour, health, household life, and the broader economy. Grounded by recent 2025–2026 evidence, researchers and organizations have documented both the mounting cost of heat and practical ways to reduce its impact. New research does not claim that every health crisis or lost workday is caused solely by climate change, but the studies show that persistent heat now shapes the day-to-day experience of thousands of informal and factory workers, families, and children across Dhaka.

FACT
Measured at the scale of the city, the region, and the nation, heat's burden falls most keenly on those least able to evade it: workers in factories and on the street, residents of less-protected housing, and children whose school routines break under the stress. While adaptation—such as improved cooling, revised work practices, and forms of social protection—can reduce some hardship, the capacity to access these benefits is not shared equally and adaptation itself is uneven.

INTERPRETATION
Causality is observed within particular factories, neighborhoods, and survey populations, which means not every effect is universally experienced or fully explained—nor can all future outcomes be predicted. The chain from exposure to outcome often crosses the boundary between work and home, from economic pressure to exhaustion and missed development opportunities for the next generation.

The Heat

Heat as an Ongoing Condition

FACT
Bangladesh’s climate, as measured in national analysis, has changed: rising heat is no longer limited to exceptional days but shapes everyday routines for millions. Heat-related physical and mental health effects in 2024 were identified in a study using over 16,000 participants, pointing to heat exposure as a persistent and cumulative condition rather than merely a series of isolated events.

WB-HEAT-2025

City, Humidity, and Human Heat Burden

FACT
In Dhaka, dense urban form and extended humidity mean that temperature alone understates the actual heat stress experienced by people. Measured at the city scale, the combination of built environment and persistent moisture in the air increases the frequency and severity of heat exposure, heightening its effects on workers and residents beyond what temperature readings alone would suggest.

The Shift

Heat at Work: Strain, Breaks, and Health

FACT
Workers within Dhaka’s garment factories report symptoms such as headaches, dizziness, fatigue, and nausea during high heat. Documented interviews and surveys show that rising temperatures directly affect workers’ need for breaks, hydration, and their overall physical capacity to maintain productivity. These cases were observed in sampled factories and among informal workers surveyed in Dhaka.

RMG-WORKER-2025 IIED-HEAT-2026

Persistent Indoor Heat

FACT
Sampling of eight Dhaka-region apparel factories revealed that indoor heat conditions persist over a prolonged season, lasting up to seven months. This sustained indoor exposure is not automatically alleviated by leaving work or by weather changes outside, but the precise nature of indoor heat stress is based on those studied factories and should not be generalized to all workplaces in Bangladesh.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026

For Informal Workers, Avoiding Heat Costs Wages

FACT
For informal outdoor workers in Dhaka, the only reliable escape from heat is to stop work. However, this causes a direct loss in paid hours, as shown by household surveys: missed time translates immediately into lost earnings, moving the cost of heat into daily decisions for these workers.

The Pay Packet

Household Income Under Heat Pressure

FACT
Surveyed informal-worker households in Dhaka face mounting financial pressure due to missed paid work during heat extremes. While not universally applicable across all of Dhaka, this pattern among the surveyed population suggests heat directly translates into tighter household budgets and increased precarity.

IIED-HEAT-2026

Heat as a National Economic Constraint

FACT
At a national scale, modeling of 2024 data found that Bangladesh lost an estimated 250 million workdays to heat-related causes. These losses aggregate the widespread, if uneven, impact of high temperatures into a significant macroeconomic constraint for the country as a whole. The number reflects a modeled estimate rather than directly observed loss by every worker.

The Home

Heat at Home: The Unending Exposure

FACT
For many Dhaka workers, heat exposure does not end when their shift is over. Evidence from both worker household surveys and sampled worker homes demonstrates that residential spaces can remain hot and uncomfortable, especially during peak heat months. The design, ventilation, and resources available in these homes further shape how much relief or continued burden families experience.

Household Capacity and Unequal Recovery

UNCERTAIN
Not every household can access effective cooling or recovery after a day of heat exposure. The current evidence shows that some families have greater resources, physical space, or access to cooling measures than others, but does not provide a complete map of household adaptation strategies or the scope of unequal burden across Dhaka.

The Next Generation

Children Caught in the Heat

FACT
Bangladesh’s 2024 heatwaves contributed to the disruption of schooling for children nationwide, as extreme heat joined other hazards in forcing school closures and affecting child health and learning outcomes. While heat was not the sole factor in all cases, the connection between rising temperatures and education interruption is directly supported by national institutional evidence.

School Closures Ripple Through Households

UNCERTAIN
Interruptions to school routines can send the burden of heat back into homes, impacting both children’s time and the resources of their families. However, the current evidence does not resolve the exact household-level effects or map the specific ways in which these interruptions affect childcare, learning, or income.

The Fix

Tested Ways to Adapt

FACT
Adaptation to heat is occurring on multiple fronts in Dhaka. Physical and operational measures documented in the evidence include increased shading, improved airflow, practical cooling interventions, provision of hydration, and adjusted work practices. These responses are based on measured interventions, and their effects have been documented in specific studies rather than as blanket solutions.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026 IIED-HEAT-2026

Payback for Cooling Investments

FACT
Evidence from sampled apparel factories in the Dhaka region shows that certain cooling investments can repay themselves by reducing lost workdays and associated costs, with estimated payback periods ranging from one to four years. These figures are specific to the studied interventions and contexts and are not guarantees of returns for all factories.

CORNELL-HEAT-2026

Who Bears the Residual Heat?

UNCERTAIN
Even as adaptation measures become more common, the ability to access such protection is uneven. Existing evidence identifies disparities in exposure and adaptation based on household resources and sector of work. The future allocation of the costs and benefits of adaptation—and who ultimately bears the residual heat burden—remains uncertain in this evidence bundle.

The Room After Work

Maya, Rafiq, Nila, their flat, dialogue, schedule decisions and events are invented. The surrounding heat, worker strain, income risk, household exposure and school disruption are evidence-established conditions; comparative/ordinary-human knowledge supports the fictional connections.

By the time Maya reaches the stairwell, the heat of the factory has changed shape but not disappeared. Outside it was traffic, concrete and wet air. Inside the flat it has been waiting in the walls.

Nila is on the floor beside the doorway with a workbook open. School has been disrupted by the heat. In this story, that means she is home before Maya expected her. The closure is grounded in documented heat-related school disruption; this family's afternoon is invented.

Rafiq has moved the small fan so it points toward Nila. Maya stands in front of it anyway for ten seconds. Nila protests. Maya laughs, then gives it back.

There is rice to wash. Maya sits first. Her shirt is still damp at the collar. At work she drank water whenever she could. She has the dull heaviness that comes after a day spent negotiating with heat.

Rafiq asks whether she will take tomorrow off if the night stays this hot. Maya asks whether tomorrow's pay will take itself off too.

It is not a speech. It is arithmetic. A missed day can mean less money. Going in can mean carrying fatigue back through the same door tomorrow night.

Nila asks whether school will open. Neither adult knows. Rafiq says they will work it out. Maya looks at the fan, then at the window, then at her daughter.

Later, after the cooking, the three of them arrange themselves around the moving air. Nila falls asleep first. Maya and Rafiq talk quietly about whether one of them can change hours if school closes again. They disagree about what is practical, then stop because they are too tired to keep disagreeing.

Nothing catastrophic happens. That is the point. The heat does not need a dramatic ending to reorganise a day. It can take a little work, a little money, a little patience and a little sleep, then come back tomorrow.

FICTION BOUNDARY

These people, dialogue and events are invented. This is not testimony.

CONSEQUENCES · FACT AND UNCERTAINTY

Heat’s Effects and the Edges of Certainty

FACT
Observed effects include widespread missed workdays, health symptoms among workers, economic losses for households, and large-scale school disruption for children. These outcomes are directly documented through national surveys, household interviews, and factory observations in 2024–2026.

WB-HEAT-2025 IIED-HEAT-2026 CORNELL-HEAT-2026 UNICEF-EDU-2025 RMG-WORKER-2025

INTERPRETATION
However, not all impacts are equally quantifiable. The full consequences for household coping, the long-run effects on children’s learning, and the future allocation of adaptation resources remain uncertain or unresolved in the current bundle. Causality cannot be firmly traced for every missed day or household adaptation: modeled losses do not equate to individual experience, and adaptation estimates are specific to studied samples rather than universal solutions.

FACT
This change is rooted in Dhaka, the capital city of Bangladesh, and its surrounding region—a dense, humid urban area where economic life, especially for workers in clothing factories and informal sectors, is highly vulnerable to sustained high temperatures. The supported evidence focuses on Dhaka’s city scale, its working households, sampled factories, and affected schools, offering city- and sometimes nation-wide, but not neighborhood-level, resolution.
