# AOC-001 — Contemporary Calama Everyday-Life Pass

**Status:** Character gate passed for a first fictional viewpoint. This file records evidence and story constraints, not a finished character.

## What residents are actually talking about

The strongest current source is the 2026 Barómetro Calama from Universidad Católica del Norte: 300 face-to-face household interviews conducted in June 2026.

Residents' conversation is not dominated by abstract copper-market theory. The survey finds everyday attention concentrated on crime, cost of living, pollution, health, migration, education and housing. When asked what had most directly affected their household during the previous year, crime and cost of living were the two leading responses.

This matters for STORY: a believable Calama household should have an ordinary life that exists independently of Atlas's AI/copper thesis.

## Mining is identity and livelihood, not a simple villain

In the same 2026 survey, 73% agreed with mining development in the region. Chuquicamata was known by 98% of respondents. Cobreloa football club was the best-known listed civic organisation, known by 90%.

Atlas should therefore avoid a simplistic "community versus mine" plot. A character can depend on, value, criticise and question mining at the same time.

## Shift life is a credible household structure

Research on mining labour in Antofagasta and Calama documents long-distance commuting and extended shift systems. UCN's 2025 account of 54 in-depth interviews reports that long absences can affect parenting, relationships and emotional wellbeing. Earlier UCN work and the documentary *Faeneros* describe the everyday split between time at the worksite and time with family.

This gives us a plausible domestic rhythm without needing to manufacture a dramatic industrial accident or protest.

## Neighbourhood life is visible

Calama has an extensive network of neighbourhood associations, housing committees, sports clubs, older-person groups and other community organisations. Current municipal records show activity across neighbourhoods including Villa Ayquina, Kamac Mayu, Villa Chuquicamata, Independencia, Nueva Alemania and others.

Recent neighbourhood projects emphasise shade, lighting, meeting rooms, playground/recreation spaces, accessibility and places for children and older residents to gather. These details are more useful for everyday fiction than generic images of an empty desert mining town.

## Civic aspirations

During Calama's 2026 ten-year municipal development-plan consultations, residents were explicitly invited to discuss social life, culture, tourism, infrastructure, public space, health, education, transport, environment, energy, safety, productive development and rural Calama.

A participant publicly described wanting clearer planning, progress on the regulatory plan and stronger future growth for the city.

The municipal 2025 account also records hundreds of cultural activities and extensive sports/community participation. Calama should therefore appear as a functioning city with recreation, schools, neighbourhood organisations, football, culture and family life—not merely an extraction outpost.

## Identity and memory

Chuquicamata's former mining camp is protected for historical, social, territorial, urban and architectural significance. Heritage authorities specifically identify its role in local identity, labour organisation and community history.

A Calama household can plausibly have family memory tied to Chuquicamata without every family member working at the mine today.

## Language and terminology worth preserving

Use naturally and only when context supports it:

- **Calameño/a** — person from Calama.
- **Loino/a** — identity term associated with Calama/El Loa; also strongly visible through Cobreloa.
- **faena** — worksite/mining operation in everyday mining usage.
- **faenero/a** — worker operating under mining/worksite shift arrangements; documented as a locally used term.
- **turno** — work shift/roster.
- **Chuqui** — possible colloquial reference to Chuquicamata, but verify contemporary usage before placing it in character dialogue.

Do not manufacture Chilean slang merely to make dialogue sound local. Dialogue needs a later native-language authenticity pass.

## What a plausible first household looks like

The evidence supports a household where mining is close enough to shape time and opportunity, but not so engineered that every member exists to explain mining.

A strong first lens would be:

**An established Calama family in which one adult works a mining-related shift job while another adult's daily life is rooted in the city, with a school-age or young-adult child whose future is not automatically assumed to be mining.**

Why this works:
- shift work gives the household two rhythms: worker present / worker away;
- the city-based adult prevents the story from becoming a mine-site story;
- the younger generation creates a natural question about staying, leaving, training, education and what Calama's next economy might be;
- family memory can connect to Chuquicamata without requiring the protagonist to be a miner;
- ordinary concerns such as prices, school, health, neighbourhood space, football, transport and family obligations can coexist with the distant global copper signal.

## Recommended protagonist lens

**Do not make the mine worker the primary protagonist.**

The more revealing Atlas viewpoint is likely the person whose life is affected by mining's rhythms without spending the story underground or at the pit.

Candidate for the next design stage:

**A Calama-based adult managing ordinary household/family life around a partner's mining roster, while thinking about a child's future in or beyond Calama.**

This is deliberately a role, not yet a name, age, gender, ethnicity, occupation or personality. Those details should emerge from the next character-design pass rather than from demographic stereotypes.

## Narrative opportunity

The distant AI signal does not need to arrive as news that the family understands.

A stronger mechanism is that the reader knows the global chain while the household experiences only local signals: talk about another project, training, overtime, contractor demand, infrastructure, prices, job prospects or a decision about whether a child should build a future around mining.

That preserves the core Atlas idea:

**global consequences can enter ordinary lives before the people living them have any reason to connect those consequences to their distant origin.**

## Guardrails

Do not:
- portray Calama as only dangerous, polluted or deprived;
- assume every family member works in mining;
- assume residents oppose mining;
- assume high mining wages equal household wealth;
- turn migration into a character trait or threat;
- use an Indigenous identity as narrative decoration;
- fabricate local slang;
- use current crime or school tragedies as plot devices;
- attribute a local change to AI without evidence.
